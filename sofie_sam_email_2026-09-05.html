Sofie, you asked in August whether we had more power now. I owe you a straight answer, and it is no. The reasons have nothing to do with your data collection. I have restructured the paper around what the data can actually support, and I think the result is a stronger paper than the one we set out to write.

1. What the paper now leads with

This is the finding, and it is solid. RRM practices essentially do not offer the services a single woman or a same-sex couple needs in order to conceive. One practice in 98 offers IUI. One offers IVF. Three work with donor sperm. Meanwhile 93 of 98 offer cycle tracking.

That is a structural barrier. The service is simply not there, which is a different and in some ways more damning claim than saying staff treat callers differently. It requires no assumptions about anyone's motives, and it comes directly from staff answering a question about their own practice.

2. Why the caller-identity comparison did not work

All three arms start at a similar width and finish at very different ones. That looks like a finding, and it may well be one, but we cannot make the claim. There are two separate reasons.

Power. A matched comparison only learns from practices that answered differently to two callers. We have 7, 2 and 8 such practices across the three comparisons. With two of them, the exact test cannot produce a significant result at any effect size. That is not "unlikely", it is arithmetically impossible. Detecting a realistic difference would take roughly 72.

Who placed the calls. I only found this one this week, and it is the harder problem.

Comparison Callers who did BOTH scenarios
Straight vs Lesbiannone
Straight vs Single mothernone
Lesbian vs Single mother3 callers, 59 calls

Nobody who made straight-couple calls also made a meaningful number of the other two kinds. Sam, your 17 calls were all straight couple; mine were too. So "straight callers did better" and "that particular caller did better" are the same column of numbers, and no statistical adjustment can separate them. Where we do have overlap, which is lesbian versus single mother, holding the caller fixed gives an odds ratio of 0.57 (0.15 to 2.15). No difference, and the same answer as the unadjusted estimate.

The awkward part is that the comparison with enough informative practices is the one with no caller overlap, and the comparison free of the caller problem is the one with too few practices to test. That is a consequence of how calls were allocated, not of anything either of you did on the phone.

3. What changed in the manuscript

  • Title and abstract now lead with the service menu.
  • Table 1 is the service menu. The access comparison moved to an explicitly exploratory section.
  • P-values removed from that section, with the reason stated: a significance test assumes a design capable of detecting the effect, and ours is not.
  • We describe the outcome as appointment availability inferred from call documentation, because the form never asked "did they offer you an appointment?"
  • The caller-allocation problem is written up as a design lesson. It is genuinely useful to other audit studies and I would like to keep it in.
