An important correction to the headline number — please read this part carefully

If you just tally the calls, single mothers look like they get scheduled more often than the other callers: about 64% vs. 40%. I don't want us to report that, because it's an artifact of which practices we called, not a real difference in how single mothers are treated. Here's the problem, step by step:

  1. We did not make a single-mother call at every practice — only at 38 of them. The other 64 practices were never dialed for that scenario.
  2. The 38 practices that did get a single-mother call happen to be the friendly, easy-to-schedule ones. We know this because those same practices also say yes to their straight and lesbian callers about 70% of the time. By contrast, the practices we never called for a single mother say yes to anyone only about 14% of the time.
  3. So the single-mother group is built almost entirely out of "yes" practices, while the straight and lesbian groups are a mix of yes-practices and all those stingy 14% practices. We're comparing single mothers at the nice offices against everyone else averaged across nice and difficult offices. Of course single mothers come out looking better — but it's the office selection doing the work, not the scenario.
  4. The fair comparison is to look within one practice at a time: for each office called as more than one scenario, did it answer its single-mother call differently than its straight/lesbian calls? Done apples-to-apples, the advantage vanishes — and single mothers actually do slightly worse, about 7 percentage points lower than the very same offices' other callers.

Bottom line: the 64% is real arithmetic but a misleading comparison. The honest read is that practices largely treat all three callers the same, with no single-mother advantage.

The honest limitation: the scenario comparison is underpowered

Here's the hard part. The within-practice test only gets information from practices that answered differently to two callers. Most practices treat everyone the same, so only 9–11 practices per comparison actually count. With numbers that small, we could only detect a very large effect (roughly a 5–6× odds difference). Realistic bias effects are far smaller, so we can rule out a big differential but cannot confirm or exclude a small-to-moderate one. Since we're not making more calls, this won't change — and that's fine, as long as we frame it honestly.

Not adequately powered: every scenario-vs-scenario comparison (acceptance and wait time). We should report these as exploratory / hypothesis-generating, with an explicit note on the power floor — not as null results.

The strongest, cleanest result: RRM practices in this sample essentially never offer IUI or IVF (1% each) and rarely work with donor sperm (3%). That's a tightly-estimated, directly relevant finding — and it stands on its own.

What I'd suggest

  1. Lead the paper with the well-powered descriptive findings (service mix, overall access, concordance) rather than the scenario contrast.
  2. Present the scenario comparison as exploratory, with the within-practice matched analysis and an explicit power-floor statement.
  3. Drop the unmatched 64% number entirely — it's confounded and a reviewer would flag it.
  4. No further calls needed for this framing. If we ever wanted to power the scenario comparison properly, it would take closer to ~100 practices per arm with complete triads, which is out of scope now.
