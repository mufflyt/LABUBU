Why we are underpowered for the scheduling question

We designed this to test whether practices schedule straight couples, lesbian couples, and single mothers differently. Four separate problems mean we cannot answer that, and they compound.

1. The paired design has almost nothing to work with. In a within practice comparison, only practices that treat two callers differently carry any information. Practices that treat both the same, in either direction, contribute nothing. Concordance was high, which is arguably reassuring, but it leaves us with very few informative practices:

ComparisonPaired practicesDiscordantSmallest detectable odds ratio at 80% power
Straight vs single mother167OR 30.9
Straight vs lesbian198OR 35.4
Lesbian vs single mother222None. No effect size is detectable.

An odds ratio of 31 is not a real world effect size in access research. Published audit studies find odds ratios in the range of 1.5 to 3. Our floor is an order of magnitude above anything anyone has ever measured, which means a null result here tells you nothing about whether an effect exists.

The last row deserves a sentence of its own. With two discordant practices, the exact McNemar test cannot produce a significant result at any effect size whatsoever. Even if every RRM practice in the country refused single mothers and accepted lesbian couples, two discordant pairs could not reach p < .05. That comparison is not weak, it is structurally incapable of returning an answer.

I should tell you that we had this wrong until recently. The software was computing the detectable effect using a normal approximation, which reported OR 8.1 and 9.0. Those numbers actually deliver about 44% power, not 80%. An independent recalculation from the exact binomial, confirmed by simulating 40,000 trials, gives the correct values above. So the study was roughly four times more underpowered than our earlier drafts admitted. Better that we found it than a reviewer.

2. Caller identity is tangled up with scenario, and no statistical adjustment fixes it. We did not randomize who called under which scenario. Cramer's V for the caller by scenario association is 0.66, which is very high. One caller placed 53 of the 77 straight couple calls and zero single mother calls. Two of the three comparisons have no caller at all who placed calls under both scenarios, which means for those comparisons the scenario column and the caller column are literally the same data. You cannot adjust for a variable that is collinear with your exposure.

We can now put a number on how much this matters. In a variance components model, the share of variance lying between callers rather than within them is:

  • 0.29 for whether a live office was reached
  • 0.31 for inferred appointment availability
  • 0.36 for the business day wait

Roughly a third of what we measured tracks who dialed the phone. That is the same order of magnitude as any scenario effect this study could have detected.

3. The scheduling outcome is derived, not measured. The REDCap instrument never asked "did they agree to schedule you?". We infer availability from whether an appointment date got written down. Throughout the paper this is called "appointment availability inferred from call documentation" rather than "the practice offered an appointment", because the second phrasing would assert something we never measured. This is named as the study's principal measurement limitation.

4. Documentation completeness differs by arm, in the same direction as the outcome. Among calls that reached staff, the appointment date is missing for 28.2% of straight couple calls, 62.2% of lesbian couple calls, and 53.1% of single mother calls. Since the outcome is read off that date, a call that was written up less completely scores as less available regardless of what the practice actually said. Differential documentation and differential availability are confounded and we cannot separate them. The direction of that asymmetry matches the direction of the exploratory finding, which is a reason to trust that finding less, not more.

What we do with the scheduling analysis

We keep it, clearly labeled exploratory, with the detectable effect floor printed next to every comparison and no p values used to support conclusions. We also report what the data exclude rather than only what we failed to detect, which is more useful. For straight versus single mother the paired difference is +31 percentage points with a 95% interval running from 7 points in the other direction to 43 points. That interval is compatible with a large disadvantage and also with no disadvantage, and saying so plainly is more honest than "no significant difference."

My view is that reporting it transparently is better than dropping it. A reader who wonders whether we looked at differential scheduling should find our answer and its limits rather than silence. If any of you disagree and would rather cut it entirely, say so and I will make the case for your version.

One instrument problem you should know about

The instrument had three checkboxes asking about "restrictions to the individuals you would provide care to". On paper that is the only direct measure of discrimination we collected. We are excluding it from every analysis and I want to explain why, because it is not a judgment about anyone's work.

The codebook defines a checked box as a restriction applying to that group. The recorded data follow two different conventions depending on who filled in the form. On some records all three boxes are checked, which under the codebook definition would mean the practice serves nobody, and clearly means the opposite. On other records only the box matching the scenario the caller was running is checked, which echoes the assignment rather than describing the practice. Both are entirely reasonable readings of an ambiguous question. The field had no note, no branching logic, and no instruction, so it invited exactly this. On top of that, a quarter of calls record no caller, so those records cannot be attributed to either convention.

The conclusion is that the item cannot be recovered by asking us what we meant, because two different conventions cannot be merged into one measurement after the fact. It is a question design failure, not a data entry failure. The fix for a future wave is three separate yes/no questions with the direction stated in the stem, made required, with the caller recorded on every record.
